Slide 2 — Microbiome Background
•	The microbiome refers to all the genetic material of microbial communities in a given environment
•	This includes bacteria, fungi, viruses and their genes — both on and inside the human body
•	We can't grow most of these organisms in a lab, so we sequence them directly from samples
•	A faecal sample, for example, gives us a window into the gut microbiota
•	We then look at the relative abundance of different microbial signatures across samples
________________________________________
Slide 3 — Two Main Sequencing Approaches
•	There are two main ways to sequence the microbiome: targeted amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomics
•	Amplicon sequencing targets a specific marker gene — most commonly the 16S rRNA gene in bacteria
•	It's cheaper and simpler but only tells you who is there, not what they're doing
•	Shotgun metagenomics sequences everything in the sample — all genomic DNA
•	It produces far more data and can give functional information, but is more expensive and computationally demanding
________________________________________
Slide 4 — Pros and Cons
•	Neither approach is perfect — the choice depends on your research question and budget
•	Shotgun metagenomics gives richer data but requires more complex analysis and is more prone to introducing bias during sample processing
•	Amplicon sequencing is simpler to QC and less prone to contamination, but primer choice can bias which organisms you detect
•	A key question with amplicon sequencing is: are we actually capturing enough variation to answer our question?
________________________________________
Slide 5 — 16S Microbiome Analysis
•	The 16S rRNA gene is the most widely used marker for studying prokaryotic diversity
•	It is present in all bacteria and archaea, making it a universal target
•	The gene contains both conserved regions — used for primer binding — and hypervariable regions (V1–V9) that differ between species
•	This variability is what allows us to tell organisms apart
•	Importantly, 16S only tells us who is there and in what relative abundance — it cannot tell us about metabolic function
•	Also be aware that some species share identical 16S sequences in certain regions, or carry multiple copies of the gene, which can complicate interpretation
________________________________________
Slide 6 — The 16S Gene
•	This slide shows the structure of the 16S rRNA gene and its variable regions
•	The graph on the right shows standardised entropy — the peaks represent the hypervariable regions V1–V9
•	Higher entropy means more variation between species, so these regions are most useful for distinguishing organisms
•	Different studies target different variable regions — V3-V4 is one of the most commonly used combinations
•	The region you choose affects which organisms you detect, which is an important source of bias to be aware of
________________________________________
Slide 7 — OTUs
•	Historically, 16S sequences were grouped into Operational Taxonomic Units, or OTUs
•	Sequences sharing a defined level of similarity — typically 97% — are clustered into the same OTU
•	There are three main approaches: de novo clustering, open reference, and closed reference
•	OTUs have limitations: closely related species can be merged together, and a single nucleotide difference can be biologically meaningful but lost in clustering
•	They are also difficult to compare across studies and hard to update when new data is added
________________________________________
Slide 8 — ASVs
•	The more modern approach is Amplicon Sequence Variants, or ASVs
•	Rather than clustering sequences, ASVs use an error model to distinguish true biological sequences from sequencing errors
•	Each unique sequence is treated as its own variant, giving much higher resolution than OTUs
•	ASVs are reproducible and can be compared across studies, and don't rely on a reference database until the classification step
•	The main drawbacks are that low-abundance species may be filtered out, and it requires more computational resources
________________________________________
Slide 9 — OTU vs ASV
•	This table summarises the key differences — ASVs are generally considered superior for most modern analyses
•	ASVs are more easily shared across studies, independent of reference bias, and capture sequences exactly rather than averaging them
•	Both approaches are still susceptible to chimeric sequences — artefacts created when two different sequences join during PCR — though ASVs handle chimera identification more simply
•	The ASV approach is now the standard and is what we will use in the practical
________________________________________
Slide 10 — Analysing the Counts Table
•	Once we have our ASV or OTU table, the real analysis begins
•	Alpha diversity looks at diversity within a single sample — how many species are there, and how evenly distributed are they?
•	Beta diversity compares diversity between samples — are two groups of people different in their microbiome composition?
•	Rarefaction helps us check whether we have sequenced deeply enough to capture the true diversity in our samples
•	Taxonomic assignment classifies each ASV to a known taxon using a reference database
•	Differential abundance analysis tests whether specific taxa are significantly more or less abundant between conditions
________________________________________
Slide 11 — Composition Analysis: A Cautionary Note
•	This is an important point that is often overlooked — microbiome data is compositional
•	We are measuring relative, not absolute, abundances — if one taxon increases, all others appear to decrease even if their true numbers haven't changed
•	This is illustrated clearly here: samples 1 and 2 look similar in raw counts, but proportionally sample 2 is more like sample 3
•	Standard statistical tests assume independence between features, which is violated in compositional data
•	Always use methods designed for compositional data, such as ANCOM or ALDEx2, and consider transformations like the centred log-ratio

Slide 12 — Practical
•	In the practical you will work through a full 16S amplicon analysis pipeline using QIIME2
•	You will start with quality control and denoising to produce clean ASVs
•	Then classify those ASVs taxonomically using a reference database
•	Apply rarefaction to normalise sequencing depth across samples
•	And finally visualise alpha and beta diversity to interpret differences between groups
