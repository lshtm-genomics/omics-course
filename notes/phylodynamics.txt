Phylodynamics & Populations — Lecture Notes

1. Introduction

Phylodynamics = study of how epidemiological, immunological, and evolutionary processes shape viral phylogenies (Volz et al. 2013)
Different viruses produce characteristically different phylogeny shapes — e.g. measles (bushy/balanced), HIV (ladder-like), influenza (rapidly evolving trunk)
Host population structure matters: structured vs. unstructured populations produce different tree topologies
Selection pressure creates imbalanced (ladder-like) trees; neutral evolution produces balanced trees


2. Molecular Dating

Molecular clock hypothesis: mutations accumulate at a roughly constant rate for a given organism/gene/strain
Two clock models:

Strict clock — all lineages evolve at the same rate
Relaxed clock — rates vary across lineages and over time


Root-to-tip regression: plots genetic distance (root-to-tip) against sampling time

Slope = substitution rate (μ = Δd/Δt)
x-intercept = time of origin (t₀)


Limitations of root-to-tip regression:

Assumes constant rate
Ignores phylogenetic non-independence
→ Better to use ML or Bayesian frameworks


Applications:

2014 Guinea Ebola outbreak: single introduction from reservoir, divergence from Central African variant ~late 2004, crossed into Sierra Leone May 2014
HIV UK: Subtype B (MSM) had shorter transmission intervals (14 months) vs. Non-B heterosexual (27 months); 25% of B transmissions within 6 months post-infection




3. Population Dynamics

Phylogenies can inform:

Effective population size (Ne) — fraction of population contributing offspring to next generation
Growth rate of epidemic
Time of origin


Coalescent theory: rate at which lineages merge going backwards in time depends on Ne and population structure

Large Ne → lineages coalesce slowly → long branches deep in tree
Small Ne → rapid coalescence → short internal branches


Skyline plots visualise Ne through time from a phylogeny
Applications:

HCV in Egypt: dramatic Ne increase mid-1900s, linked to contaminated antischistosomiasis injections
Zika in Central America: birth-death skyline estimated Re > 1 during 2015–2016 epidemic wave; spread from Honduras outward
Phylogenies can also directly estimate numbers of infectious vs. removed individuals through time




4. Phylogeography

Reconstructs spatial spread of pathogens by mapping geographic location onto phylogeny branches
Parsimony approach: assigns ancestral locations minimising total state changes — fast but ignores phylogenetic uncertainty and asymmetric transition rates
Bayesian MCMC approach (preferred):

Integrates over phylogenetic uncertainty
Uses molecular clock + demographic priors
Calculates probability of each ancestral location given observed data


Applications:

H5N1 in SE Asia: origin traced, spread animated across time snapshots
H3N2 influenza globally: SE Asia identified as major source of annual flu strains
2014 Ebola: detailed dispersal routes mapped at district level within Guinea/Sierra Leone/Liberia

Slide 6 — Section Header: Molecular Dating

Slide 7 — The Molecular Clock Hypothesis

Core idea: for a given organism, gene, or strain, mutations accumulate at a roughly constant rate over time
This allows genetic distance to be converted into calendar time
Evidence shown: HIV divergence from founder sequence increases linearly over ~10 years post-seroconversion (Shankarappa 1999) — supports clock-like behaviour within hosts
Two clock models:

Strict clock: every lineage in the tree evolves at exactly the same rate — simple but often unrealistic
Relaxed clock: rates are allowed to vary across branches and over time — more realistic, especially for datasets spanning long time periods or multiple viral lineages


Ebolavirus example (Dudas & Rambaut 2014): genetic distance increases linearly with year of collection across outbreaks from 1976–2014 — demonstrates clock-like evolution between hosts
The slope of that line = the substitution rate


Slide 8 — Estimating Substitution Rates

Method: Root-to-Tip linear regression
Setup:

Collect sequences sampled at different time points (heterochronous sequences)
Build a phylogeny — branch lengths are in units of genetic distance (substitutions per site)
For each sequence (tip), measure: dᵢ = genetic distance from root to tip, tᵢ = calendar date of sampling


Plot dᵢ (y-axis) against tᵢ (x-axis) → should give a straight line if clock-like
Slope (μ) = evolutionary rate in substitutions/site/year
x-intercept (t₀) = estimated time of origin (when distance = 0, i.e. the root)
Linear equation: y = μx + a
Tool commonly used: TempEst (formerly Path-O-Gen) for visualising root-to-tip regression
Important: this is a quick, exploratory method — has significant limitations (addressed on next slide)


Slide 9 — From Genetic Diversity to Time

Once you have a substitution rate, you can convert a genetic distance tree into a time tree
The x-axis changes from substitutions/site → calendar years
This "dated phylogeny" (chronogram) allows you to:

Date the origin of an epidemic — the root of the tree corresponds to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of all sampled sequences
Date internal nodes — these correspond to branching events, e.g. when a new outbreak or cluster began
Estimate transmission intervals — the length of internal branches in time units approximates the interval between transmission events


Example shown: HIV phylogeny converted from genetic distance to a time tree spanning ~1995–2005
Red dashed arrow on time tree = uncertainty interval around node date estimates


Slide 10 — Statistical Inference of Evolution Rates

Root-to-tip regression has two major unrealistic assumptions:

Constant evolution rate across all lineages and time — violated if rate varies
Independence of data points — violated because sequences share evolutionary history (internal branches are not independent)


Better approach: incorporate phylogenetic structure directly into statistical inference
Method:

Each tip is assigned its known sampling time
Internal nodes are given starting times consistent with their order in the tree (later nodes must be younger than earlier ones)
The vector of node times and substitution rate are jointly optimised
Find parameter values giving the best statistical fit to observed sequence data


Two main frameworks:

Maximum likelihood (ML): finds the single set of parameter values maximising the likelihood of the data — fast but no uncertainty estimates
Bayesian MCMC: samples from the posterior distribution of parameters — gives full uncertainty estimates (credible intervals), can incorporate prior knowledge


Software: BEAST (Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis by Sampling Trees) is the dominant tool

Slide 13 — Section Header: Population Dynamics

Slide 14 — Phylogenies & Population Dynamics

Beyond timing, phylogenies encode information about historical population sizes
Three key demographic parameters estimable from sequence data:

Effective population size (Ne): the number of individuals in a population who actually contribute genetic material to the next generation — in viral epidemiology, this approximates the number of actively transmitting infections
Growth rate: how fast the epidemic was expanding (or contracting)
Time of origin: when the epidemic began


Workflow shown:

Multiple aligned sequences → phylogenetic tree → population size through time (sigmoid growth curve shown)


The shape of the branching pattern encodes population history:

Many recent coalescent events (lots of branching near the tips) → rapid recent growth
Few deep coalescent events (long branches near root) → small ancestral population




Slide 15 — The Coalescent Theory

Coalescent theory (Kingman 1982): a mathematical framework describing how gene lineages merge (coalesce) going backwards in time
Key relationship: rate of coalescence ∝ 1/Ne

Large population → low coalescence rate → long waiting times between merges → long branches
Small population → high coalescence rate → short waiting times → short branches, rapid merging


Population structure also matters — subpopulations slow down coalescence across demes
Skyline plot: graphical representation of Ne through time, inferred from the pattern of coalescent intervals in a dated phylogeny

Left example: Ne increasing toward present → growing epidemic
Right example: Ne decreasing toward present → declining epidemic


Reference: Lemey et al. 2007 AIDS Rev


Slide 16 — The Coalescent Theory II (diagram)

Visual illustration of the coalescent process
Reading left (present) to right (past): viral population shrinks from many particles at t₀ to a single ancestor at t₋₈
The phylogeny (top) mirrors this: many tips at the present coalesce into fewer and fewer lineages going back in time
Key insight: when the population was large, coalescent events are spread out over many time intervals; when population was small, lineages merge quickly
Internal nodes of the phylogeny correspond to transmission events — their timing and density reveal population dynamics


Slide 17 — The Coalescent Theory II (annotated)

Same diagram now annotated with epidemiological interpretations of the skyline:

Number of new infections at the present (left/present end of skyline) = current epidemic size
Rate of growth (slope of the skyline) = how fast the epidemic expanded or contracted
Origin of the epidemic (right/past end of skyline) = when the founding lineage first appeared


The red line (effective population size skyline) rises steeply from a low value in the past → indicates exponential epidemic growth
This connects directly to R₀ and epidemic doubling time — rapid coalescence growth rate can be converted to epidemiological growth parameters


Slide 18 — Hepatitis C Virus in Egypt

Classic example of coalescent analysis revealing historical epidemic dynamics
Data: 63 HCV E1 gene sequences from Egypt (Pybus et al. 2003; Drummond et al. 2005)
Finding: Ne was very low before ~1918, then dramatically increased in the mid-20th century
Proposed explanation: large-scale parenteral antischistosomiasis treatment campaigns in Egypt (1920s–1980s) used inadequately sterilised needles → massive iatrogenic HCV transmission
Egypt has one of the highest HCV prevalence rates in the world — this analysis helped explain why
The 1918 vertical line marks the estimated timing of the Ne increase
Demonstrates that phylodynamics can uncover historical public health events not recorded in clinical data
Uncertainty intervals (dashed lines) around skyline = Bayesian credible intervals


Slide 19 — Estimating Epidemiological Parameters

More sophisticated models can go beyond Ne to estimate explicit epidemiological parameters directly from phylogenies
Birth-death models: model the phylogeny as the result of a branching process where:

Birth = transmission event (new infection)
Death = removal from infectious pool (recovery, death, treatment)


Diagram shows: infectious individuals (blue) and removed individuals (orange) estimated simultaneously through time from a viral phylogeny

Branching events in phylogeny (purple) correspond to transmission events
Tips (sampling events) correspond to removals


This allows direct estimation of:

Transmission rate (β)
Removal/recovery rate (γ)
R = β/γ (basic reproductive number)


Reference: du Plessis & Stadler 2015 Trends in Microbiology


Slide 20 — Estimating Zika Re in Central America & Mexico

Application of birth-death skyline model to the 2015–2016 Zika epidemic
Reference: Thézé et al. 2018, Cell Host & Microbe
Phylogeny (panel A): colour-coded by country of origin, showing multiple introductions and spread across Central America
Key phylogeographic finding: ZIKV spread outward from Honduras to other Central American countries
Re (effective reproductive number) through time (bottom panel) shows four distinct phases:

Early spread within Honduras — Re rising, lineages diversifying
Rapid radiation across Central America — Re peaks
Pre-epidemic period — Re fluctuating around 1, virus spreading silently before cases detected
Observed epidemic peak (April–July 2016) — Re elevated, corresponds to reported case surge


Re > 1 = epidemic growing; Re < 1 = epidemic declining (red dashed line = Re of 1)
Demonstrates genomic epidemiology detecting epidemic spread before clinical surveillance


Slide 21 — Section Header: Phylogeography

Slide 22 — Reconstructing Geographic Dispersal

Phylogeography: reconstruction of the geographic history of pathogen spread using phylogenies
Core idea: assign a geographic location to each branch of the phylogeny — not just the sampled tips, but also inferred ancestral nodes
Example shown (France/Spain/UK): sequences from three countries — phylogeny used to infer which country the pathogen was in at each point in history
Parsimony approach:

Ancestral state reconstruction minimising the total number of location changes across the tree
Quick and intuitive
Limitations: ignores phylogenetic uncertainty (treats one tree as truth), ignores that some transitions may be more likely than others (e.g. Spain→France more likely than Spain→UK)


Reference: Pybus & Rambaut 2009 Nature Reviews Genetics


Slide 23 — Reconstructing Geographic Dispersal (Bayesian)

Bayesian MCMC approach overcomes parsimony limitations:

Samples over many possible phylogenies — accounts for phylogenetic uncertainty
Models asymmetric transition rates between locations (A→B ≠ B→A)
Integrates geographic dispersal model with molecular clock and demographic model simultaneously
Output: probability distribution over ancestral locations at each node — not just a single answer


Two main implementations:

Discrete phylogeography: locations are discrete categories (countries, cities) — uses continuous-time Markov chain model
Continuous phylogeography: locations are GPS coordinates — models diffusion across landscape


Calibrated under strict/relaxed clock models and coalescent or birth-death demographic priors
Software: BEAST with BEAGLE library for computational efficiency

Slide 10 — Statistical Inference of Evolution Rates

Root-to-tip regression has two major unrealistic assumptions:

Constant evolution rate across all lineages and time — violated if rate varies
Independence of data points — violated because sequences share evolutionary history (internal branches are not independent)


Better approach: incorporate phylogenetic structure directly into statistical inference
Method:

Each tip is assigned its known sampling time
Internal nodes are given starting times consistent with their order in the tree (later nodes must be younger than earlier ones)
The vector of node times and substitution rate are jointly optimised
Find parameter values giving the best statistical fit to observed sequence data


Two main frameworks:

Maximum likelihood (ML): finds the single set of parameter values maximising the likelihood of the data — fast but no uncertainty estimates
Bayesian MCMC: samples from the posterior distribution of parameters — gives full uncertainty estimates (credible intervals), can incorporate prior knowledge


Software: BEAST (Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis by Sampling Trees) is the dominant tool
Why move beyond regression: root-to-tip regression treats each sequence as an independent observation, which they are not — sequences from the same clade share much of their evolutionary history. Ignoring this produces biased rate estimates and overconfident timing inferences
Why Bayesian over ML: Bayesian MCMC propagates uncertainty through the entire analysis — you get credible intervals on every parameter including node dates, substitution rates, and population sizes. This is essential for honest scientific inference, especially in outbreak response where decisions are made under uncertainty

Slide 16 — The Coalescent Theory II (diagram)

Visual illustration of the coalescent process
Reading left (present) to right (past): viral population shrinks from many particles at t₀ to a single ancestor at t₋₈
The phylogeny (top) mirrors this: many tips at the present coalesce into fewer and fewer lineages going back in time
Key insight: when the population was large, coalescent events are spread out over many time intervals; when population was small, lineages merge quickly
Internal nodes of the phylogeny correspond to transmission events — their timing and density reveal population dynamics
Why visualising the coalescent process helps: it makes concrete the abstract link between what we observe (branch lengths in a phylogeny) and what we want to infer (historical population size). The diagram shows intuitively why a large population produces long branches — there are simply more individuals for a lineage to "wait" before finding a common ancestor


Slide 17 — The Coalescent Theory II (annotated)

Same diagram now annotated with epidemiological interpretations of the skyline:

Number of new infections at the present (left/present end of skyline) = current epidemic size
Rate of growth (slope of the skyline) = how fast the epidemic expanded or contracted
Origin of the epidemic (right/past end of skyline) = when the founding lineage first appeared


The red line (effective population size skyline) rises steeply from a low value in the past → indicates exponential epidemic growth
This connects directly to R₀ and epidemic doubling time — rapid coalescence growth rate can be converted to epidemiological growth parameters
Why extracting these three parameters is practically important:

Current epidemic size informs resource allocation
Growth rate informs urgency of intervention and helps calculate R₀
Time of origin informs retrospective investigation — where and when to look for the index case or animal reservoir event




Slide 18 — Hepatitis C Virus in Egypt

Classic example of coalescent analysis revealing historical epidemic dynamics
Data: 63 HCV E1 gene sequences from Egypt (Pybus et al. 2003; Drummond et al. 2005)
Finding: Ne was very low before ~1918, then dramatically increased in the mid-20th century
Proposed explanation: large-scale parenteral antischistosomiasis treatment campaigns in Egypt (1920s–1980s) used inadequately sterilised needles → massive iatrogenic HCV transmission
Egypt has one of the highest HCV prevalence rates in the world — this analysis helped explain why
The 1918 vertical line marks the estimated timing of the Ne increase
Uncertainty intervals (dashed lines) around skyline = Bayesian credible intervals
Why this example is so important methodologically: it demonstrates that phylodynamic analysis can recover a historical public health event (the treatment campaigns) from sequence data alone, with no prior knowledge of that event built into the model. The genomic signal was strong enough to implicate a cause that epidemiological records had not conclusively established
Why Bayesian credible intervals must be shown: the wide uncertainty intervals remind us that 63 sequences give limited statistical power to resolve fine-scale demographic history — honest reporting of uncertainty is essential to avoid overinterpreting results


Slide 19 — Estimating Epidemiological Parameters

More sophisticated models can go beyond Ne to estimate explicit epidemiological parameters directly from phylogenies
Birth-death models: model the phylogeny as the result of a branching process where:

Birth = transmission event (new infection)
Death = removal from infectious pool (recovery, death, treatment)


Diagram shows: infectious individuals (blue) and removed individuals (orange) estimated simultaneously through time from a viral phylogeny

Branching events in phylogeny (purple) correspond to transmission events
Tips (sampling events) correspond to removals


This allows direct estimation of:

Transmission rate (β)
Removal/recovery rate (γ)
R = β/γ (basic reproductive number)


Why birth-death models are an advance over coalescent models: the coalescent is a neutral model — it doesn't explicitly model transmission and recovery as separate processes. Birth-death models are mechanistically grounded in epidemiological theory, meaning the parameters you estimate (β, γ, R) are directly interpretable in public health terms rather than being proxies like Ne
Why estimating R from genomic data matters: R is the single most important parameter in epidemic management, but it is difficult to estimate from case counts when surveillance is incomplete. Genomic estimation of R is independent of case ascertainment and can therefore provide a cross-check on surveillance-based estimates
Reference: du Plessis & Stadler 2015 Trends in Microbiology


Slide 20 — Estimating Zika Re in Central America & Mexico

Application of birth-death skyline model to the 2015–2016 Zika epidemic
Reference: Thézé et al. 2018, Cell Host & Microbe
Phylogeny (panel A): colour-coded by country of origin, showing multiple introductions and spread across Central America
Key phylogeographic finding: ZIKV spread outward from Honduras to other Central American countries
Re (effective reproductive number) through time (bottom panel) shows four distinct phases:

Early spread within Honduras — Re rising, lineages diversifying
Rapid radiation across Central America — Re peaks
Pre-epidemic period — Re fluctuating around 1, virus spreading silently before cases detected
Observed epidemic peak (April–July 2016) — Re elevated, corresponds to reported case surge


Re > 1 = epidemic growing; Re < 1 = epidemic declining (red dashed line = Re of 1)
Why tracking Re through time rather than assuming a constant R₀: epidemic dynamics change — interventions are introduced, population immunity builds, behaviour changes. A static R₀ gives you a single snapshot. Re through time tells you whether the epidemic was accelerating or decelerating at each moment, and whether interventions had measurable impact
Why the detection of pre-epidemic spread (phase 3) is significant: it shows the virus was circulating silently at Re ≈ 1 for months before case counts triggered an alarm. This demonstrates that genomic surveillance can detect epidemics earlier than clinical surveillance — particularly important for arboviruses where symptoms are non-specific and case ascertainment is poor


Slide 21 — Section Header: Phylogeography

Slide 22 — Reconstructing Geographic Dispersal

Phylogeography: reconstruction of the geographic history of pathogen spread using phylogenies
Core idea: assign a geographic location to each branch of the phylogeny — not just the sampled tips, but also inferred ancestral nodes
Example shown (France/Spain/UK): sequences from three countries — phylogeny used to infer which country the pathogen was in at each point in history
Parsimony approach:

Ancestral state reconstruction minimising the total number of location changes across the tree
Quick and intuitive
Limitations: ignores phylogenetic uncertainty (treats one tree as truth), ignores that some transitions may be more likely than others (e.g. Spain→France more likely than Spain→UK)


Why we need to reconstruct ancestral locations at all: we only have sequences from sampled individuals at known locations — we don't directly observe where the virus was at intermediate time points. Ancestral state reconstruction is the statistical method that lets us infer the unobserved geographic history of unsampled lineages
Why parsimony alone is insufficient: it finds the most parsimonious explanation but gives no measure of confidence, cannot account for unequal transition probabilities between locations, and treats phylogenetic uncertainty as if it doesn't exist — all of which can lead to confidently wrong conclusions about spread pathways
Reference: Pybus & Rambaut 2009 Nature Reviews Genetics


Slide 23 — Reconstructing Geographic Dispersal (Bayesian)

Bayesian MCMC approach overcomes parsimony limitations:

Samples over many possible phylogenies — accounts for phylogenetic uncertainty
Models asymmetric transition rates between locations (A→B ≠ B→A)
Integrates geographic dispersal model with molecular clock and demographic model simultaneously
Output: probability distribution over ancestral locations at each node — not just a single answer


Two main implementations:

Discrete phylogeography: locations are discrete categories (countries, cities) — uses continuous-time Markov chain model
Continuous phylogeography: locations are GPS coordinates — models diffusion across landscape


Calibrated under strict/relaxed clock models and coalescent or birth-death demographic priors
Software: BEAST with BEAGLE library for computational efficiency
Why asymmetric transition rates matter: viral spread between locations is rarely symmetric. A major international hub may seed many countries (high outgoing rate) while rarely receiving introductions (low incoming rate). Forcing symmetric rates misspecifies the model and can reverse inferred directionality of spread — critically important when trying to identify source populations
Why joint estimation of clock + demography + geography is better than sequential estimation: doing each step separately propagates only point estimates forward, discarding uncertainty. Joint Bayesian estimation propagates full uncertainty from sequence data all the way through to geographic conclusions — giving more honest and calibrated inference


Slide 24 — Influenza A H5N1 Dispersal in South East Asia

Reference: Lemey et al. 2009 PLoS Computational Biology
Application of Bayesian discrete phylogeography to avian influenza H5N1
Left panel: time-resolved phylogeny colour-coded by geographic location (province/country level)

Gs/GD = Goose/Guangdong lineage — the ancestral source lineage
Multiple distinct colour clusters = multiple geographic introductions and local persistence


Right panels: animated snapshots of viral dispersal (May 1997 → 2001 → 2003 → 2005)

Circles = estimated location of ancestral lineages (size = uncertainty)
Lines = inferred dispersal routes between locations
Shows progressive spread across SE Asia over time


Key finding: China (particularly Guangdong region) was the primary source of H5N1 spread across SE Asia
Why identifying source populations matters for control: targeted surveillance and culling/vaccination interventions are most efficient when directed at true source populations. Without phylogeographic analysis, every affected region appears equally important — with it, you can prioritise where to focus limited veterinary and public health resources
Why animating spread over time is informative: static maps show where virus has been but not the sequence or speed of spread. Animated phylogeography reveals the temporal order of introductions, which is essential for evaluating whether border control or movement restrictions could have been effective and at what time points


Slide 25 — Global Migration of Human Influenza A H3N2

Reference: Bedford et al. 2010 PLoS Pathogens
Bayesian phylogeographic analysis of seasonal influenza H3N2 on a global scale
Top panel: large time-resolved phylogeny colour-coded by global region — strong ladder shape confirms selection driving a single trunk lineage
Trunk = the lineage that gives rise to all future diversity — identifying where the trunk is tells you where the global source population is
Bottom panel (trunk probability over time): shows which region was on the trunk at each time point

SE Asia and China dominate the trunk probability — identified as the primary global source of seasonal influenza
USA, Europe, Oceania have periodic trunk contributions but are largely sink populations — they receive virus but don't seed the global epidemic


Why identifying source vs. sink populations matters for vaccine strain selection: WHO influenza vaccine strain recommendations must anticipate which strains will circulate 6–12 months into the future. Knowing that SE Asia/China is the global source means surveillance there should be prioritised and weighted more heavily in vaccine composition decisions — a directly actionable public health finding
Why the source-sink dynamic is counterintuitive: the USA and Europe have the most sophisticated influenza surveillance systems and report the most cases numerically — yet phylogeography shows they are epidemiological sinks, not sources. This highlights how case burden and epidemiological importance are not the same thing, and why genomic data is needed to reveal true transmission hierarchies


Slide 26 — Early EBOV Dispersal in the 2014 Epidemic

Reference: Dudas et al. 2017, Nature
High-resolution Bayesian phylogeography of early Ebola spread at the district level within West Africa
Left panel (phylogeny): tips labelled by district and country; branches colour-coded by inferred location

Guéckédou (Guinea) = origin district, confirmed as the epicentre
Distinct clades for Guinea (GN), Sierra Leone (SLE), Liberia (LBR)


Right panel (map): arrows show inferred dispersal routes between districts

Initial spread within Guinea from Guéckédou → Kissidougou, Macenta, Conakry, Siguiri
Cross-border spread: Guinea → Kailahun (Sierra Leone) and Guinea → Lofa (Liberia)


Posterior probability distributions (bottom left) show timing uncertainty for key dispersal events
Key finding: early epidemic driven by a small number of long-distance dispersal events rather than continuous spread
Why district-level resolution matters: country-level phylogeography would have told us Guinea preceded Sierra Leone and Liberia — useful but not actionable. District-level resolution identified which specific border crossings and movement corridors transmitted the virus, informing where to deploy contact tracing teams, establish checkpoints, and focus community engagement
Why identifying long-distance jumps vs. continuous spread changes the response: if spread were predominantly short-range (village to village), a ring containment strategy centred on known cases would work well. The finding that spread was driven by occasional long-distance jumps (likely along major transport routes) indicated that movement surveillance and border monitoring were equally important as local ring vaccination — fundamentally altering the strategic response


Slide 27 — Summary

The full phylodynamic workflow, from raw sequences to biological insights:

StepInputModel UsedOutputWhy this step1Pathogen gene sequencesNucleotide substitution modelEvolutionary treeConverts raw sequences into a branching history of relatedness2Evolutionary treeMolecular clock modelDated evolutionary treeAnchors genetic relationships in calendar time3Dated treeDemographic modelPopulation dynamics / NeExtracts epidemic size and growth history4Dated treeTranslocation modelGeographic dispersal historyReconstructs where and when spread occurred

Each layer adds biological interpretation — no single step alone gives the full picture
Modern tools (especially BEAST) perform steps 2–4 jointly in a single Bayesian analysis — parameters are co-estimated, reducing bias
Why the pipeline must be followed in order: each step depends on the output of the previous one. A dated tree is meaningless without a reliable clock model; population dynamics inference is unreliable without accurate node dates. Skipping or poorly executing any step propagates error through all subsequent analyses
Reference: Pybus & Rambaut 2009 Nature Reviews Genetics — recommended reading for overview of the field
