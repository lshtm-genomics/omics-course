Slide 2 — Course Outline (first view)

The course follows a logical pipeline starting from raw sequencing data
We begin with alignment — mapping reads to a reference genome
From the alignment we assess coverage — how well the genome is represented
Finally we catalogue genomic variants — the differences between our sample and the reference
An alternative route is de novo assembly, where we reconstruct the genome without a reference

Slide 4 — Omics Cascade

The term 'omics' refers to the comprehensive study of a particular class of biological molecule
The cascade starts at the genome — the complete DNA sequence of an organism
DNA is transcribed into RNA, giving us the transcriptome — which genes are being expressed
RNA is translated into proteins, giving us the proteome — which proteins are being produced
Proteins drive metabolism, generating metabolites that make up the metabolome
All of these layers ultimately influence phenotype — what an organism looks and behaves like
In this course we will focus primarily on the genome and transcriptome, highlighted in the diagram
We will also touch on the microbiome — the community of microorganisms associated with a host


Slide 5 — Sanger Sequencing

Before next-generation sequencing, all genomic data was generated using Sanger or capillary sequencing
This first-generation technology was used to sequence the first human genome, the M. tuberculosis H37Rv reference, and the P. falciparum 3D7 malaria genome
Sanger sequencing produces long reads of around 700 base pairs, which is an advantage
However it required cloning of DNA fragments, was expensive at around $500 per megabase, produced low coverage, and took months to complete a genome
Despite its limitations it was transformative — it gave us our first reference genomes, which we still use today


Slide 6 — Platform Comparison Table

Next-generation sequencing has revolutionised genomics by dramatically increasing throughput and reducing cost
This table compares several sequencing platforms available today
Illumina platforms dominate the field — the HiSeq X can produce up to 900 gigabases in 3 days at just $7 per gigabase
PacBio and Oxford Nanopore produce much longer reads — up to 20kb and 200kb respectively — at the cost of lower throughput and higher per-gigabase cost
Compared to Sanger sequencing, modern platforms represent a more than 10,000-fold increase in data per run, a 1,000-fold drop in cost, and a 10 million-fold increase in throughput
The choice of platform depends on your research question — short reads for variant calling, long reads for assembly and structural variation


Slide 7 — Next Generation Platforms

This slide highlights three of the key platforms you will encounter throughout the course
Oxford Nanopore's MinION is a portable, pocket-sized device capable of generating reads hundreds of kilobases long — it was famously used for sequencing in remote field settings
Pacific Biosciences uses single molecule real-time sequencing to generate long reads of more than 10 kilobases, and can also detect DNA methylation directly
Illumina's HiSeq X Ten — a set of ten ultra-high-throughput instruments — brought the cost of sequencing a human genome down to $1,000 for the first time
All three platforms have their strengths and we will encounter data from several of them during the course


Slide 8 — Platform Comparison (Nature Protocols)

This comprehensive table from Nature Protocols gives a broader overview of sequencing platforms and their applications
454/Roche was one of the first NGS platforms but has since been discontinued — it was widely used for bacterial and viral genome sequencing
Illumina remains the most widely used platform for RNA-seq, genome-wide association studies, and clinical applications
ABI SOLiD and Ion Torrent offer alternatives but are less commonly used in current infectious disease research
Pacific Biosciences is particularly valuable for methylation detection and complex genome assembly
Oxford Nanopore stands out for its portable form factor and suitability for pathogen surveillance and field sequencing
The key takeaway is that no single platform is best for everything — understanding the tradeoffs is important when designing a study


Slide 9 — Sequence Read Archive

All next-generation sequencing data generated in publicly funded research is deposited in publicly accessible databases
The two main archives are the NCBI Sequence Read Archive and the European Nucleotide Archive at the EBI
These databases contain petabytes of sequencing data covering thousands of organisms and studies
Being able to access and reuse public data is a core skill in bioinformatics — many important discoveries have been made by reanalysing existing datasets
During this course we will work with data downloaded from these archives as well as data generated locally


Slide 10 — The Course (raw data highlighted)

Now let's look at the first step of the pipeline in more detail — raw sequence data
This is what comes off the sequencing machine — millions of short DNA sequence reads stored in fastq format
Before we can do anything useful with it, we need to understand what it looks like and how to assess its quality


Slide 11 — Raw Sequence Data (fastq)

Illumina sequencing works by fragmenting DNA into pieces of around 300 base pairs and sequencing both ends — these are called paired-end reads
Each fragment generates a Read 1 and a Read 2, separated by the insert — the unsequenced middle portion
The sequencer produces between 50 and 100 million of these short fragments, each around 100 base pairs long
The data is stored in fastq format — each read consists of four lines: an identifier, the DNA sequence, a separator, and a quality score string
The quality scores encode the confidence the sequencer has in each base call
The central challenge is: how do we reconstruct a genome from millions of these tiny fragments?


Slide 12 — Alignment to a Reference

The answer — when a reference genome is available — is alignment
Each read is compared to the reference genome and placed at the position it most likely came from
This produces a pile-up view, as shown here using the LookSeq visualisation tool
You can see how millions of short reads stack up along the reference, with paired reads shown as connected pairs
This is the foundation for everything that follows — coverage analysis, variant calling, and structural variant detection


Slide 13 — The Course (alignment highlighted)

We now move to the alignment step in the pipeline
The output of alignment is a set of files — SAM, BAM, and pileup files — that we will work with throughout the practicals
These files contain not just the sequence but information about mapping quality, position, and read orientation


Slide 14 — Central Dogma and Sequencing

This diagram brings together the central dogma of molecular biology with the sequencing workflow
Part A shows the flow from DNA to RNA to protein, with methylation as an additional layer of regulation influencing gene expression
Part B shows how sequencing fits in — DNA is extracted and sequenced, producing raw reads which are then aligned to a reference
From the alignment we can call variants — positions where our sample differs from the reference — producing a table of positions, reference bases, and alternate bases
This is the core of genomic epidemiology — identifying variants that distinguish strains, confer drug resistance, or affect virulence


Slide 15 — Alignment and Beyond

This slide summarises the full pipeline from raw data to variant lists, showing the files, tools, and quality control steps at each stage
Raw fastq files are checked for quality using FastQC, screened for contamination using fastq_screen, and problematic reads removed using Picard tools
Alignment tools include BWA, Smalt, Bowtie, and Novoalign — we will use BWA in the practicals
The alignment produces SAM and BAM files, which are assessed for mapping rate and genome coverage using samtools flagstat
Variant calling uses tools like samtools mpileup for SNPs and indels, and Pindel, CNVnator, or Breakdancer for structural variants
The final output is a VCF file — a standardised format listing all variants with quality scores and genotype information


Slide 16 — FastQC Quality Scores

FastQC is one of the first tools we run on any new dataset — it gives a rapid overview of read quality
The plot shows quality scores at each position along the read — the higher the score, the more confident the base call
Q20 means 1 error per 100 bases, Q30 means 1 per 1000, and Q40 means 1 per 10,000
You can see that quality tends to drop towards the end of reads — this is characteristic of Illumina sequencing
Reads showing poor quality towards the end can be trimmed using Trimmomatic — this is called hard clipping
Alternatively, alignment software can soft-clip low-quality bases during the mapping step itself


Slide 17 — Types of Variants

Once we have aligned reads, we can detect several types of genetic variation
SNPs — single nucleotide polymorphisms — are single base changes, such as an A changing to a G
Indels are insertions or deletions of one or more bases — even a single base indel can disrupt a protein's reading frame
Copy number variation involves larger duplications or deletions — sometimes spanning entire genes as shown here with gene B
Each type of variant requires different tools and filtering strategies to detect reliably


Slide 18 — Pile-up View: Detecting SNPs

This is a real example of a SNP detected in a Lisboa strain of M. tuberculosis
In the pile-up view you can see a C to T change at a single position within the Rv0245 gene
The vast majority of reads show T at this position, giving us high confidence this is a true variant rather than a sequencing error
This kind of view is also useful for detecting mixed infections — if two different strains are present, you would see roughly equal numbers of reads supporting each allele at heterozygous positions


Slide 19 — VCF Format

After variant calling, results are stored in VCF — Variant Call Format
Each row represents a single variant, with columns for chromosome, position, reference allele, alternate allele, and quality score
Additional fields contain information about read depth, mapping quality, and genotype calls
VCF files can represent data from a single sample or multiple samples simultaneously — multi-sample VCFs are particularly useful for comparing strains in a population
These files are generated from BAM files using Samtools and BCFtools, which we will use in the practicals


Slide 20 — Using Paired End Mapping

Paired-end sequencing is particularly powerful for detecting structural variants like large deletions
This example shows a deletion in the Pfrbp2b gene in P. falciparum
In the region of the deletion there is zero coverage — no reads map there
However, read pairs that span the deletion show an abnormally large insert size — the two reads are much further apart than expected
This discordant read pair signal, combined with the coverage drop, allows us to confidently call large deletions even when individual reads don't span the entire deleted region


Slide 21 — Using Read Depth

Read depth or coverage is another powerful signal for detecting structural variation
Low or zero coverage at a region suggests that the sequence is deleted in the sample
Excess coverage — significantly more reads than expected — suggests that a region has been duplicated
This example shows the MDR1 gene in a malaria isolate from Thailand compared to the 3D7 reference
The Thai isolate shows dramatically elevated coverage over this region, indicating amplification of the MDR1 gene — a known mechanism of drug resistance in malaria


Slide 22 — The Course (de novo highlighted)

When mapping to a reference is not possible or appropriate, we turn to de novo assembly
This is particularly important for novel organisms, highly divergent strains, or regions of the genome that cannot be reliably mapped


Slide 23 — What if We Cannot Map to a Reference?

Mapping breaks down in regions of high repetition — as shown here in the Rv1587/8c repeat region of an M. tuberculosis Lisboa strain
Reads from repetitive regions map to multiple locations, making it impossible to place them accurately
The solution is de novo or reference-free assembly — reconstructing the genome sequence directly from the reads without using a reference
We will cover de novo assembly in detail later in the course


Slide 24 — The Course (visualisation highlighted)

Throughout the course we will use visualisation software to inspect our data at every stage
Being able to look at your alignment, your coverage, and your variants visually is essential for quality control and biological interpretation


Slide 25 — Tablet

Tablet is a genome browser designed specifically for viewing next-generation sequencing alignments
It displays the coverage track at the top, showing how many reads cover each position
Below that is the read panel — the individual reads laid out against the reference
On the left you can navigate between chromosomes or contigs
The gene track shows annotated features, and nucleotide and amino acid sequences can be viewed at high zoom
Tablet is fast, easy to use, and works well for large BAM files


Slide 26 — Artemis

Artemis is a more fully featured genome browser developed at the Sanger Institute
It displays coverage by region at the top, making it easy to spot deletions or duplications
It uses inferred mapping distance — the span between paired reads — to help visualise insertions and deletions
Clicking on individual reads gives detailed mapping information including mapping quality, flags, and CIGAR string
Artemis is particularly well suited to bacterial genome analysis and annotation, and we will use it in several of the practicals
